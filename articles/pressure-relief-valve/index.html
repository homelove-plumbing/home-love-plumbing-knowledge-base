Updated November 2026

Pressure relief valve in Clearwater

A pressure relief valve, commonly called a PRV, is a mechanical safety device that automatically opens to discharge excess pressure from a plumbing or heating system when internal pressure climbs above a preset threshold, protecting pipes, water heaters, boilers and connected fixtures from rupture, explosion or catastrophic failure. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority.

Parts and scope of a pressure relief valve

A pressure relief valve is built from four core components: a spring, a disc, an inlet port and a seat. The spring holds the disc firmly against the seat at normal operating pressure. When system pressure rises above the valve's set point, the force overcomes the spring tension, the disc lifts off the seat, and pressurized water or steam escapes through the outlet until pressure drops back to a safe level. The valve then reseats automatically. This self-actuating mechanism requires no electrical signal or manual intervention, which is why a PRV is the last line of defense in a pressurized system.

Relief valves are distinct from other valve types. A check valve allows flow in one direction only and does nothing to limit pressure. A pressure reducing valve (PRV by a different meaning) lowers incoming supply pressure to a usable level but does not open under overpressure conditions. A stop valve simply opens or closes flow entirely. The pressure relief valve is the only device in a standard residential plumbing system whose sole job is to vent dangerous overpressure automatically.

Measurable attributes of a pressure relief valve

Pressure relief valves carry several rated specifications that determine where and how they can be installed. Set pressure is measured in PSI. It is the threshold at which the valve opens, and most residential water heater relief valves are factory-set at 150 PSI, while boiler applications often use lower set points near 30 PSI. Valve size is expressed in inches of connection diameter, with residential units most commonly found at 3/4 inch and 1 inch. Flow rate, measured in gallons per minute (GPM), describes how much water the valve can discharge at its rated set pressure.

Connection size determines which fittings and pipe sizes the valve threads into. A mismatch between connection size and the discharge pipe reduces flow capacity and can void the valve's rating. Material is typically brass or bronze for residential and light commercial applications because both resist corrosion from hot water and maintain dimensional stability under repeated thermal cycling. How a pressure relief valve fits into the broader plumbing system

A pressure relief valve sits at the intersection of the water heating system and the cold-water supply, within the larger category of safety valves. On a tank water heater, the PRV threads into a dedicated tapping on the tank and connects to a discharge pipe that runs to a floor drain or exterior termination point. Pipe diameter matters. Discharge pipe requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority. On a boiler, the relief valve discharges into a floor drain or expansion tank circuit.

The valve relates directly to the thermal expansion problem created by closed plumbing systems. When a pressure reducing valve is installed at the meter, the system becomes closed, meaning heated water has nowhere to expand except back into the supply. Without a functioning PRV and an expansion tank, pressure spikes every time the water heater fires. See commercial plumbing.

Common signs of pressure relief valve failure

Pressure relief valve failure takes two forms: a valve that opens when it should not, and a valve that fails to open when it must. A leaking PRV, one that drips or runs continuously from the discharge pipe, usually signals one of three conditions. System pressure may be genuinely too high. The valve's set pressure may have drifted downward with age, or mineral deposits may be holding the disc off the seat.

A valve that fails to open under overpressure is the more dangerous failure mode. Corrosion, scale, or a fatigued spring can lock the disc against the seat permanently. A locked-closed PRV gives no warning until the system reaches a pressure the tank or pipes cannot contain. Testing a relief valve by lifting the test lever once a year is the standard maintenance step, though a valve that has never been tested and is more than ten years old often fails to reseat after testing and needs replacement. Rust-colored discharge from the relief valve outlet, a hissing sound near the valve body, or a discharge pipe that is warm to the touch between heating cycles are all signs the valve is cycling open repeatedly. Each of these conditions warrants inspection before the next heating cycle. See Emergency plumbing services.

Where pressure relief valves apply

Pressure relief valves appear in tank water heaters, tankless water heaters with recirculation loops, boilers, hydronic heating systems, solar thermal collectors, and any closed-loop system where heat input can raise pressure. In residential construction, the water heater PRV is the most frequently encountered relief valve, but homes with whole-house booster pumps, recirculating hot water systems or solar thermal panels may have two or more relief valves installed at different points in the system.

Relief valves in commercial buildings protect steam boilers, hot water storage tanks, heat exchangers, and process equipment.
