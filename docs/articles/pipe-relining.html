Pipe Relining: A Home Love Plumbing Reference Guide

Updated November 2026

Pipe relining in Clearwater

Pipe relining, known as Cured-in-Place Pipe (CIPP), serves as a trenchless approach for fixing damaged pipes. The technique inserts a resin-saturated liner into the existing pipe and cures it on site to create a new structural pipe wall within the old one. The process handles sewer lines, drain lines, supply lines and stormwater pipes sized from 50 mm to 300 mm without any excavation of the ground above the pipe.

Pipe relining as a type of trenchless pipe repair

Pipe relining belongs to the category of Trenchless Pipe Repair. These methods restore damaged pipes from the inside instead of digging them up. Traditional pipe replacement demands a trench along the entire length of the damaged line. Pipe rehabilitation creates a new structural surface inside the host pipe from one or two access points. The completed liner bonds to the interior wall and carries flow on its own, so the host pipe functions only as a casing. Scope and component parts of pipe relining

Pipe relining is a type of trenchless pipe repair. The process includes four stages: diagnosis, preparation, liner installation, and curing. These stages combine to restore a damaged pipe run. The main components consist of an epoxy resin liner, a calibration tube known as an inflatable bladder, the resin, a curing agent, and the curing equipment that hardens the liner. The epoxy resin liner is cut to match the length of the damaged section, saturated with resin, then inverted or pulled into place inside the host pipe with inversion equipment. The inflatable bladder presses the wet liner against the pipe wall. It maintains that position while the curing agent activates. Curing equipment then supplies UV light, steam, or hot water to set the resin according to the liner material and site conditions.

  • Epoxy resin liner: Epoxy resin liner forms the new pipe wall and bonds directly to the interior surface of the host pipe.
  • Calibration tube: Calibration tube holds the liner against the pipe wall under pressure during the cure cycle.
  • Resin: Resin saturates the liner fabric and hardens into a rigid, watertight shell once activated.
  • Curing agent: Curing agent triggers the chemical reaction that converts the wet resin into a solid structural liner.
  • Curing equipment: Curing equipment delivers UV light, steam or hot water to the liner to complete the cure within the specified time window.

Pipe relining carries defined, measurable specifications. These specifications decide whether a given pipe qualifies and how the job is scoped. Pipe diameter compatibility extends from 50 mm to 300 mm, equal to roughly 2 inches to 12 inches, which covers most residential drain and sewer lines. Liner thickness ranges from 3 mm to 6 mm according to pipe diameter, degree of structural damage, and liner material selected. Cure time ranges from 2 hours to 8 hours, with UV-cured liners typically finishing faster than steam- or hot-water-cured systems. Three primary curing methods exist: UV light, steam, and hot water. Each method suits different liner materials and access conditions. Liner material consists most commonly of fiberglass-reinforced felt saturated with epoxy resin, though polyester felt liners appear in lower-pressure drain applications.

A camera inspection of the pipe before relining confirms the diameter, identifies the damage type and rules out sections that are too collapsed to accept a liner. Pipes with more than 30 percent cross-sectional deformation generally require spot excavation or full replacement before a liner can be installed. Drain line camera equipment helps assess whether a pipe qualifies for relining before any work begins. Leak detection equipment is frequently used alongside camera systems for diagnosis.

Pipe relining versus traditional pipe replacement and excavation

Pipe relining differs from traditional pipe replacement and excavation in cost, timeline and property impact. Traditional pipe replacement requires digging a trench along the full length of the damaged line, removing the old pipe, laying new pipe and backfilling. The work can disturb landscaping, driveways, concrete slabs and foundations. Cured-in-Place Pipe avoids that excavation by operating through existing access points such as cleanouts or small entry pits and leaves the surrounding ground undisturbed. The finished liner adds a smooth, joint-free interior surface that resists root intrusion and corrosion better than many original pipe materials. Reduced downtime benefits commercial plumbing applications where a trench across a parking lot or loading area can halt operations for days. Many repairs can incorporate pipe corrosion remediation through this method.

Common problems and signs that pipe relining applies

Pipe relining applies to pipes with root intrusion, corrosion, pinhole leaks, cracked joints, offset joints and minor collapse that still retain enough structural shape to accept a liner. Slow drains that return after repeated cleaning indicate root intrusion or a cracked joint that snaking cannot permanently fix. Recurring wet spots in a yard, unexplained increases in water bills and sewage odors near a slab or foundation suggest a damaged line that a camera inspection can confirm. Pipe rehabilitation does not suit pipes that have fully collapsed, sections with severe offset joints that block liner passage or pipes outside the 50 mm to 300 mm diameter range. Where pipe relining services apply

Pipe relining applies to sewer lines, drain lines, stormwater pipes, and some potable water supply lines. These applications cover residential homes, commercial buildings, and municipal infrastructure. In residential settings, the most common uses involve main sewer lines running from the house to the street and branch drain lines under concrete slabs where excavation requires breaking finished flooring. Commercial applications differ in scale. Cured-in-Place Pipe restores larger-diameter drain and sewer lines under parking structures, warehouses, and multi-story buildings where access is limited and downtime is expensive. The method works on clay, cast iron, PVC, concrete, and orangeburg pipe and therefore fits the range of pipe materials found in Clearwater older and newer construction.
